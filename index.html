    Abstract

    Why do consumers in some countries pay many times more for gasoline than consumers in others? This paper examines how state capacity and government shielding shape the retail price of gasoline across 114 countries from 2016 to 2025. Using Prais–Winsten regression with panel-corrected standard errors, it finds that the price consumers pay is shaped most by who owns the oil sector and by the type and quality of government. Countries with a national oil company pay about 26 cents less per liter than comparable countries without one. More effective governments charge consumers more, about 5 cents per liter for each 10-point increase in government effectiveness, consistent with the view that capable states tax fuel rather than subsidize it, and more democratic governments charge more as well. Price regulation lowers prices on its own, but its effect weakens once regional differences in fuel policy are taken into account. Currency depreciation lowers the dollar price of fuel, while elections, net importer status, and currency pegs have no independent effect. The findings suggest that state ownership of the oil sector, rather than formal price controls, is the most durable source of cheap fuel.

    Introduction

    The price of gasoline has returned to the center of economic and political debate. After a decade of volatile world oil markets, governments turn to fuel taxes, subsidies, and price controls as tools of revenue, protection, and political survival, and the price consumers pay at the pump now ranges from about 12 cents to more than two dollars per liter from one country to another (Ross, Hazlett, and Mahdavi 2017). The question that follows is why. A large body of research explains how pump prices move when world oil prices change, finding that they rise faster than they fall and adjust over weeks or months (Borenstein, Cameron, and Gilbert 1997; Kpodar and Abdallah 2017). While the mechanism of that change may be understood from a global perspective, far less is understood on the difference of price levels from one country to another. This paper asks: what explains cross-national variation in the price consumers pay for gasoline?

    The question is new in two respects. First, research on fuel prices is largely concerned with movement rather than level, and it treats the pump price as the product of market forces: world crude costs, competition, and the pricing choices of refiners and retailers (Borenstein, Cameron, and Gilbert 1997; Weyl and Fabinger 2013). The one large cross-national study of pass-through finds that political factors have no short-run effect on how world prices reach the pump (Kpodar and Imam 2021). Second, the cross-national research that does compare price levels has studied one channel at a time, asking either why governments subsidize gasoline or why they tax it, but not how state capacity, government shielding, and the structure of the fuel market and currency work together (Cheon, Urpelainen, and Lackner 2013; Hammar, Löfgren, and Sterner 2004). It is understood that a world oil price shock arrives from outside the country, but the price level at the pump itself doesn’t. Instead, those prices are set over years, through policies a government writes, collects, and can choose to offset. The difference between those three mechanisms is the reason to study politics in relation to shaping the price level, even where it does not shape the short-run pass-through of world prices.

    This paper treats the path from the world price of gasoline to the pump price as a sequence of political and economic steps. The world price of gasoline, set where global supply meets global demand, determines the cost of the fuel itself. A government then decides how much to add to that cost through taxation, a choice that depends on its capacity to collect revenue at all (Besley and Persson 2009, 2014). The government may also choose to subtract from it, holding prices down by decree, directing state-owned firms to absorb costs, or timing relief around the moments when voters are watching (Cheon, Lackner, and Urpelainen 2015; Klomp 2020). Beneath these steps lie the structural conditions of each country's fuel market and currency, which determine what imported fuel costs in local terms and what consumers pay when that price is measured in dollars (Goldberg and Knetter 1997; Gopinath et al. 2020). At each step the state can widen or narrow the gap between the world price and the price consumers pay. In this sense, that gap is a measure of what the state is able to, and chooses to carry out (Fukuyama 2004).

    Gasoline is a useful place to study this gap. It is a homogeneous and widely traded product with a public world benchmark price, so the share of the pump price that comes from policy rather than from the cost of the fuel can be measured with unusual precision. It is also one of the most politically sensitive prices consumers face. Governments of every type manage fuel prices closely, in part because they fear the unrest and electoral costs that rising fuel prices can bring (Kim and Urpelainen 2016; Klomp 2020). Nearly every country publishes a retail gasoline price, meaning gasoline offers a comparable outcome across very different states.

    The answer to the question of price level differneces for three reasons. First, it matters for policy. Fuel subsidies cost governments hundreds of billions of dollars each year, while fuel taxes are among the most cost-effective tools for raising revenue and limiting emissions, so a government that does not understand what sets its pump price cannot anticipate the fiscal or environmental effects of its own choices (Coady et al. 2017; Sterner 2007). Second, it matters for those who bear the costs and benefits of fuel policy. Low prices benefit the households that consume the most fuel, drain the treasury, and are difficult to reverse once consumers come to expect them (Fattouh and El-Katiri 2013; Hammar, Löfgren, and Sterner 2004). Third, it matters for the study of the state. The pump price turns a familiar economic quantity into an observable, price-based measure of the distance between what governments decide and what they deliver, a distance that political scientists usually measure through expert surveys and perception indices (Kaufmann, Kraay, and Mastruzzi 2010; Besley and Persson 2011). This makes it uniquely useful in studying the efficacy of government types and the priorities of those structures, as well.

    Using a panel of 114 countries from 2016 to 2025, this paper finds that the price consumers pay is shaped most by who owns the oil sector and by the type and quality of government. Countries with a national oil company pay about 26 cents less per liter than comparable countries without one, while more democratic and more effective governments charge consumers more. Elections, trade position, and currency pegs show no independent effect, and the effect of price regulation weakens once regional differences in fuel policy are taken into account.

    Literature Review

    The dependent variable in this study is the price of gasoline, defined as the retail price per liter that consumers pay at the pump, measured in US dollars. The literature agrees on the basics: the pump price is the world cost of the fuel plus whatever taxes, margins, and subsidies are added or subtracted before it reaches the consumer (Coady et al. 2017; Ross, Hazlett, and Mahdavi 2017). It disagrees, however, about how to measure it and what drives it. Much of the fuel price research measures prices in local currency and studies how they change after a shock, finding that world crude prices reach the pump unevenly and over weeks or months (Borenstein, Cameron, and Gilbert 1997; Kpodar and Abdallah 2017). Work that compares levels across countries instead measures prices in a common currency and treats the gap between the pump price and the world benchmark as a measure of net taxation or subsidy (Cheon, Urpelainen, and Lackner 2013; Ross, Hazlett, and Mahdavi 2017). A second disagreement concerns the source of that gap. One line of work treats it as the product of market structure and competition (Weyl and Fabinger 2013; Marion and Muehlegger 2011); another treats it as a policy choice shaped by fiscal need, political pressure, and resource wealth (Hammar, Löfgren, and Sterner 2004; Cheon, Urpelainen, and Lackner 2013). This paper measures the price of gasoline in US dollars per liter, averaged over each year, rather than in local currency or adjusted for purchasing power, because the question concerns what consumers pay compared across countries (Burke and Nishitateno 2013). Converting every price to a common currency makes countries directly comparable, but it comes at a cost: dollar prices move with exchange rates as well as with fuel policy, so a country’s pump price can fall in dollar terms even when nothing about its taxes or subsidies has changed. This paper accounts for this by controlling for changes in each country’s exchange rate against the US dollar.

    The first concept is state capacity, measured here through a government effectiveness variable. Scholars broadly agree that state capacity is a government's ability to plan and carry out policy, and to enforce the laws it passes (Fukuyama 2004). They also agree that fiscal, legal, and administrative capacity tend to be built together rather than separately (Besley and Persson 2009, 2011). There is a disagreement on how to measure it, however. Some studies use broad governance indices; others use corruption indices or measures specific to a single agency, such as the customs service, and these are not interchangeable predictors of the same outcome (Kellenberg and Levinson 2019; World Bank 2023). Broad measures of governance also carry measurement error and are closely tied to national income. Their anchoring to income raises the concern that they capture how rich a country is rather than how well it is governed (Kaufmann and Kraay 2002; Kaufmann, Kraay, and Mastruzzi 2010), a concern that has led some scholars to build measures designed to separate capacity from development and regime type (Hanson and Sigman 2021). This paper defines government effectiveness as the quality of public administration and the state's capacity to implement policy, as captured by the Worldwide Governance Indicators. The broad measure is preferred because the theory requires a state that can both collect revenue on fuel and administer the programs that would replace fuel subsidies, two tasks a single agency measure would only partly capture.

    The second concept is government shielding, defined as the tools a government can use to keep the cost of fuel from reaching consumers. This idea is measured through three variables, the first being price regulation. Researchers agree that governments that set or cap retail fuel prices control how much of any cost increase reaches consumers, and that prices follow world costs more closely where pricing is flexible (Kpodar and Imam 2021). Studies differ in whether they classify pricing regimes as fixed country traits or as policies that change over time, because pricing policy both advances and backslides within countries (Ross, Hazlett, and Mahdavi 2017), the time-varying approach is preferred where data allow. Data on pricing regimes are available for only part of the period studied, so this paper codes price regulation as whether a country regulated retail fuel prices at any point in that window and treats it as a stable trait. The second idea is state ownership of the oil sector. There is broad agreement that state oil companies serve political as well as commercial goals and are used to hold down domestic fuel prices (Victor, Hults, and Thurber 2012; Ichsan, Lockwood, and Ramadhani 2022). Some work measures this through the state's share of refining capacity, which captures how much of the market the government can direct; most cross-national work measures it through the presence of a national oil company (Cheon, Lackner, and Urpelainen 2015). This paper follows the second approach, because it is available for every country studied. The third and final government shielding concept is government type, where the literature is most divided. One line of work finds that autocracies, which depend on the support of urban populations, subsidize petroleum products more than democracies (Kim and Urpelainen 2016). Another finds that democracies, and especially majoritarian democracies, favor consumers over producers and lower consumer prices (Rogowski and Kayser 2002; Chang, Kayser, and Rogowski 2008; Chang et al. 2011; Weinberg 2012; Baker and Wojcik 2019). A third finds that political leadership has little effect on fuel pricing at all (Martinez-Alvarez et al. 2022; Kpodar and Imam 2021), while recent work suggests that democracy lowers fuel price controls only when it is paired with institutional capacity (McAlexander, Yang, and Urpelainen 2025). These findings may be reconciled if different regimes shield consumers through different tools. This paper measures government type with a continuous electoral democracy index rather than a democracy/autocracy dichotomy, so that differences of degree are not hidden, and uses a four-category regime classification as an alternative.

    In a competitive market with no government intervention, the pump price of gasoline should equal the world cost of the fuel plus the costs of refining, transport, and retail, and should therefore be roughly the same across countries once converted to a common currency (Weyl and Fabinger 2013; Marion and Muehlegger 2011). Differences in price levels must then come from something that interrupts the path from the world market to the pump. The literature points to two political interruptions. First, the state may add to the price by taxing fuel, which depends on its capacity to collect revenue. Second, the government may subtract from the price by shielding consumers through price regulation, state-owned oil companies, or the incentives that come with its type of government. A third, economic interruption, the structure of the fuel market and the currency, can also change what imported fuel costs consumers before any policy is applied; this paper accounts for it through control variables and the additional variables reported in Appendix B. Because the price level is set over years through policies the government controls, the political interruptions should matter more for the level of the price than for its short-run response to world price shocks, which arrive regardless of what the state does (Kpodar and Imam 2021).

    Government effectiveness is expected to be the strongest of these explanations, and it is expected to operate through taxation. Fuel is one of the easiest goods for a government to tax, because it passes through a small number of refineries, ports, and wholesalers, and effective states are better able to collect what they levy (Besley and Persson 2009, 2014). Effective states also have other ways to support poor households, such as targeted transfers, and so have less reason to hold the price of fuel down for everyone (McAlexander, Yang, and Urpelainen 2025; Cheon, Urpelainen, and Lackner 2013). Where the state is weak, it collects less from fuel and relies more on cheap fuel as a form of social support, so prices should be lower. The price of gasoline should therefore rise with government effectiveness.

    Price regulation is expected to be the strongest of the shielding variables. Where the government sets the retail price, it decides directly how much of the world cost consumers pay, and the price can sit well below world levels even when the fuel itself is fully imported (Kpodar and Imam 2021; Ross, Hazlett, and Mahdavi 2017). This is also why price regulation is essential to the model. Without it, a government that holds prices down by decree would look the same as a government that simply taxes fuel lightly. Because capable states also regulate prices, the two must be distinguished rather than conflated (Cheon, Urpelainen, and Lackner 2013).

    State ownership of the oil sector offers a second route to shielding. State owned oil companies can be instructed to absorb cost increases and hold prices below cost, as occurred when state marketing companies ran losses under administered pricing (Victor, Hults, and Thurber 2012; Ichsan, Lockwood, and Ramadhani 2022). Cross-national evidence finds that national oil companies halve the effect of oil price increases on domestic gasoline prices, and that this holds even among oil importers with capable bureaucracies (Cheon, Lackner, and Urpelainen 2015). The variable is viable because it captures shielding that operates through firms rather than through formal price rules.

    Government type is expected to be the weakest and most contested of the shielding variables. Autocrats, fearing urban unrest, subsidize petroleum products more than democrats do (Kim and Urpelainen 2016). Yet democracies are also found to favor consumers, and democratization lowers consumer prices in part through trade policy (Chang et al. 2011; Rogowski and Kayser 2002; Weinberg 2012; Baker and Wojcik 2019). Other work finds little effect of political leadership on fuel pricing at all (Martinez-Alvarez et al. 2022). The variable is retained because the shielding literature consistently points to less democratic regimes as the most active subsidizers of gasoline, but its effect may be absorbed by the more direct shielding tools above.

    Hypotheses

    Hypothesis 1: Given greater government effectiveness, there will be an increase in the price of gasoline.

    The first hypothesis concerns state capacity and follows the taxation mechanism. A state with weak administration collects less revenue of every kind, and fuel taxes are among the first a weak state gives up or never builds. Weak states also lack the tools to support poor households directly, so they rely on cheap fuel as a broad form of social support. As government effectiveness rises, more of the levy on fuel is collected and less of the price is held down, so the price of gasoline should rise.

    Hypothesis 2: Given government-regulated fuel prices, we expect a decrease in the price of gasoline.

    The second hypothesis concerns the most direct form of government shielding. Where the government fixes retail fuel prices or adjusts them through a formula it controls, the pump price responds to political decisions rather than to world costs. A government that does not wish consumers to bear the full cost of fuel can simply decline to raise the regulated price. The price of gasoline should therefore be lower under regulated pricing than under free pricing.

    Hypothesis 3: Given state ownership of the oil sector, we expect a decrease in the price of gasoline.

    The third hypothesis concerns shielding through state-owned firms. When the state owns an oil company, it can direct that firm to absorb costs rather than pass them on, covering its losses through the budget. This allows the government to shield consumers without formally controlling prices. The price of gasoline should therefore be lower where the state owns a national oil company.

    Hypothesis 4: Given a less democratic government type, we expect a decrease in the price of gasoline.

    The fourth hypothesis concerns the type of government. Autocratic leaders depend on keeping order in the cities, where rising fuel prices can spark unrest, and electoral autocracies face the added pressure of periodic elections without the full accountability of democracy. These regimes have both the motive and the means to keep fuel prices low. The price of gasoline should therefore be lower under a less democratic government. Because some evidence finds that democracies also favor consumers, this hypothesis is held with less confidence than the others.

    Data and Methods

    Data for this study are based on a panel of countries observed each year from 2016 to 2025. The data were first assembled as a monthly panel covering 207 economies from January 2015 to December 2025 and then averaged to one observation per country per year. Using the country-year as the unit of analysis permits estimating the relative influence of state capacity, government shielding, and market and currency structure on the price of gasoline while holding world prices constant within each year. Retail gasoline prices are available for 132 countries, and the main model includes 1,013 country-years from 114 countries for which every variable is observed. The sources and measures for every variable are described in Appendix Table A1.

    To measure the price of gasoline, this paper turns to the World Bank's Global Fuel Prices Database (World Bank 2026a). The database reports monthly retail gasoline prices from December 2015 to April 2025, in whatever currency and unit each country publishes: some countries report prices in local currency per liter, others in cents, per gallon, or in euros or US dollars. For each country, the series the World Bank flags as its default was used, which is the regular-grade series where one exists and premium otherwise. Each series was first converted to the main currency unit per liter and then to US dollars using monthly average exchange rates from the International Monetary Fund (International Monetary Fund 2026), and the monthly dollar prices were averaged within each year. Unfortunately for the analysis, a small number of country-years report dollar prices that reflect exchange rate problems rather than real prices. For example, Lebanon's prices from 2021 to 2023 were converted at an official rate during the collapse of its currency, and Liberia's reported price in 2024 and 2025 suddenly rose to about four times its usual level. To correct for this, these five country-years were removed before analysis.

    For the first hypothesis, government effectiveness is measured with the government effectiveness score from the Worldwide Governance Indicators (World Bank 2026d), which ranges from 0 to 100 and captures perceptions of the quality of public services, the civil service, and the formulation and implementation of policy. To measure price regulation, this paper turns to the World Bank's Global Fuel Subsidies and Price Control Measures Database (World Bank 2026b). The database records whether each country's retail fuel prices are set by the government or by the market, but only for the years 2021 through 2024. To extend the measure across the full period, price regulation is coded 1 if a country regulated retail fuel prices in any of those years, and it is treated as a stable trait of each country. State ownership of the oil sector comes from the Natural Resource Governance Institute's National Oil Company Database (Natural Resource Governance Institute 2026), and a country is coded 1 if it appears in the database. One adjustment was made: Denmark is coded 0, because its listed company left the oil and gas business in 2017.

    Government type comes from the Varieties of Democracy project (Coppedge et al. 2026) and is measured with the V-Dem electoral democracy index, which ranges from 0 to 1. The four-category regime classification from the same source serves as an alternative.

    Six control variables are included in the main model. GDP per capita at purchasing power parity comes from the World Development Indicators (World Bank 2026c) and is logged, to account for the higher price levels of richer countries (Balassa 1964). The world gasoline price is the US Gulf Coast gasoline spot price reported by the U.S. Energy Information Administration (2026), converted to dollars per liter and averaged over the same months as each country's pump price, to account for the common shocks all countries face each year (Kpodar and Abdallah 2017). Sanctions come from Release 5 of the Global Sanctions Data Base (Felbermayr et al. 2020; Global Sanctions Data Base 2026), and a country is coded 1 if it was under a United States, European Union, or United Nations sanction that year. Refining capacity, in barrels per day, comes from the same World Bank database as price regulation (World Bank 2026b) and is logged after adding one, so that countries with no refineries are kept, to account for a country's ability to supply its own fuel (Burke and Nishitateno 2013). Currency depreciation is calculated from the same IMF exchange rates used to convert pump prices (International Monetary Fund 2026), as the yearly change in the log of local currency units per US dollar, to account for the effect of exchange rate movements on dollar prices (Kpodar and Imam 2021). Finally, a set of regional indicators following the World Bank's regional groupings, with Europe and Central Asia as the base category and Malta grouped with Europe, accounts for regional traditions of fuel taxation and subsidy for which no comparable cross-national data exist (Sterner 2007; Fattouh and El-Katiri 2013). For the alternative measure of government effectiveness, the Corruption Perceptions Index from Transparency International (2026) is used.

    To test the hypotheses presented above, this paper estimates a model of the price of gasoline that can be summarized as follows:

    GPit = α + β1GEit + β2PRi + β3NOCi + β4Eit + β5Dit + β6NIit + β7PEGit + β8Yit + β9Wit + β10Sit + β11Rit + β12Cit + γREGi + εit

    where GP is the price of gasoline in country i and year t; α is a constant; β₁ through β₁₂ are the coefficients to be estimated; GE is government effectiveness; PR is price regulation and NOC is state ownership of the oil sector, both of which do not change over time and so carry only the country subscript; E is election timing; D is government type, measured as the democracy index; NI is net importer status; PEG is currency regime; Y is the log of GDP per capita; W is the world gasoline price; S is sanctions; R is the log of refining capacity; C is currency depreciation; REG is a set of regional indicators, with γ its vector of coefficients; and ε is an error term.

    Before estimation, the data were checked for the problems common to data that follow the same countries over time. The dependent variable is close to normally distributed, with a skewness of 0.120 and a kurtosis of 2.500. However, the full model estimated with ordinary least squares (reported as Model 3 in Appendix Table A2) showed that the size of its errors differs across countries (Breusch-Pagan χ² = 24.640, p < .001; Levene W0 = 2.950, p < .001), and that each country's error in one year strongly predicts its error in the next (coefficient = 0.886, p < .001). These tests are reported in Appendix Table A3. Ordinary least squares would therefore understate the uncertainty of every estimate. For this reason, all models are estimated using Prais-Winsten regression with panel-corrected standard errors, which allow the errors to differ in size across countries and to be correlated across countries in the same year, and with a common first-order autocorrelation term to correct for the persistence of errors over time (Beck and Katz 1995, 2011). The panel is unbalanced, so the error covariances are computed using all years each pair of countries shares. Country fixed effects are not used, because two of the hypothesis variables, price regulation and state ownership, do not change within a country over the period studied and would be absorbed by them (Plümper, Troeger, and Manow 2005). Multicollinearity is not a concern: no variance inflation factor exceeds 10, and the highest, for the log of GDP per capita, is 8.130.

    The analysis centers on two models. Model 1 includes all four hypothesis variables and three additional variables (a national election, net importer status, and a pegged currency) with only the two basic controls, GDP per capita and the world price. Model 2, the main model, includes the same variables and all controls. Models 5 through 11, which test each variable separately with all controls included, are reported in Appendix B, along with the theory behind the three additional variables. Two further models, reported in Appendix A (Models 3 and 4), check whether the results depend on the correction for serial correlation or on the choice of measures.

    Findings

    The results support three of the four hypotheses and suggest that the price consumers pay for gasoline is shaped more by who owns the oil sector and what kind of government holds power than by how prices are formally regulated. The findings are discussed in order of significance in the full model.

    State ownership of the oil sector has the strongest and most consistent effect, which supports Hypothesis 3. Countries with a national oil company pay about 26 cents less per liter than comparable countries without one, and the effect holds in every model, including the robustness checks in the Appendix. This follows the literature, which finds that national oil companies halve the effect of world oil price increases on domestic gasoline prices (Cheon, Lackner, and Urpelainen 2015) and that state oil firms are used to absorb costs and hold prices below market levels (Victor, Hults, and Thurber 2012; Ichsan, Lockwood, and Ramadhani 2022). This paper extends that finding from the movement of prices to their level. It also departs from the expectation stated above that price regulation would be the strongest shielding tool. Shielding that operates through state-owned firms appears to matter more than shielding through formal price rules.

    Government type has the second strongest effect, and the results support Hypothesis 4: less democratic governments charge lower prices for gasoline. This places the paper on one side of the most divided debate in the literature. The results agree with work finding that autocracies subsidize petroleum products more than democracies (Kim and Urpelainen 2016), and do not support work finding that democracies favor consumers by lowering prices (Rogowski and Kayser 2002; Chang et al. 2011; Weinberg 2012; Baker and Wojcik 2019). One reconciliation is that those studies concern prices of goods shaped by trade protection, where democracies lower prices by lowering barriers, while fuel prices are shaped by subsidies, which autocracies use to buy stability. The results also do not support work finding that political leadership has little effect on fuel pricing (Martinez-Alvarez et al. 2022; Kpodar and Imam 2021); that work, however, concerns short-run changes in prices rather than their level. The effect of democracy holds while government effectiveness is held constant, which is consistent with work suggesting that regime type and institutional capacity each shape fuel pricing (McAlexander, Yang, and Urpelainen 2025). The alternative four-category measure in the Appendix shows that every more democratic regime type has higher prices than closed autocracy, but it does not show that electoral autocracies have the lowest prices, so the expectation that electoral autocracies subsidize the most is not supported here.

    Government effectiveness has the third strongest effect and is positive, which supports Hypothesis 1. As the taxation mechanism predicts, more effective states charge more for gasoline, not less. This follows evidence that countries with weak institutions subsidize gasoline (Cheon, Urpelainen, and Lackner 2013) and that weak states raise little revenue from any source (Besley and Persson 2014). The result also speaks to the concern that broad governance measures capture national income rather than governance (Kaufmann and Kraay 2002; Hanson and Sigman 2021). In the full model, government effectiveness remains significant while GDP per capita does not, and the corruption measure used as an alternative produces the same result, so the effect does not appear to be a proxy for wealth.

    Price regulation is negative and significant when tested alone (Appendix Table B1) and alongside the other hypothesis variables, but not once regional controls are added, so Hypothesis 2 is only partly supported. Regulated prices are concentrated in particular regions, especially the Middle East and North Africa and parts of Asia, and the regional indicators appear to absorb much of their effect. This is consistent with work suggesting that fuel price control clusters with regime type and institutional capacity (McAlexander, Yang, and Urpelainen 2025). The result may also reflect measurement: price regulation is observed only from 2021 to 2024 and treated as a stable trait, even though pricing policy both advances and backslides within countries (Ross, Hazlett, and Mahdavi 2017).

    Currency depreciation, included as a control, has one of the largest effects in the model: a 10 percent fall in a currency's value is associated with a price about 2 cents per liter lower in dollar terms. This is consistent with evidence that depreciation slows the pass-through of world fuel prices (Kpodar and Imam 2021) and that exchange rate changes reach retail prices only partly (Campa and Goldberg 2005).

    Taken together, the findings suggest that the political explanations matter more than the economic ones, as the theory expected. Two of the three significant variables are political choices about the structure of the state, who owns the oil sector and how democratic the government is, and the third is the state's capacity to carry out policy. The economic explanations, discussed in Appendix B, matter mainly through the value of the currency rather than through trade position or currency regime.

    Conclusion

    This paper asked what explains cross-national variation in the price consumers pay for gasoline. Across 114 countries from 2016 to 2025, the answer lies mainly in the structure of the state. Countries with a national oil company pay substantially less, more democratic governments charge more, and more effective governments charge more as well. Elections, trade position, and currency regime have no independent effect, while price regulation matters less than expected once regional patterns are taken into account.

    These findings carry three larger implications. First, for policy, they suggest that the most durable form of fuel subsidy is not a formal price control but state ownership of the oil sector, which hides the cost of cheap fuel inside the accounts of a state firm rather than in the budget. Reformers who focus on removing price controls may leave the larger source of low prices in place. Second, for who bears the costs and benefits of fuel policy, the results suggest that cheap fuel is a feature of less democratic and less capable states, where it serves as a broad and costly substitute for the targeted support that capable states can provide. Third, for the study of the state, the positive effect of government effectiveness suggests that the pump price does work as an observable measure of what governments are able to deliver: capable states tax fuel, while weaker states give it away.

    The next research steps follow from the limits of this design. Price regulation should be measured as a policy that changes over time rather than as a fixed trait, which would allow a sharper test of whether it shapes prices independently of region. Election timing should be measured by month rather than by year, and tested alongside state capacity and the closeness of the race. Data on fuel taxes themselves would allow the regional indicators used here to be replaced with direct measures of taxation and subsidy. Finally, the original motivation for this project, how gasoline import tariffs pass through to consumer prices, remains open. Testing it would require event-level data on tariff changes and the legal instruments that impose them, including their political design and legislative complexity, which could build on the price-level findings reported here.

    Additional Hypotheses

    Literature and Theory

    Election timing is a further form of government shielding, and it captures when shielding is most likely. Cross-national and subnational studies find that governments expand fuel support and cut fuel taxes before elections, particularly when prices are high (Klomp 2020; Adhikari, King, and Vadlamannati 2026), and that pump prices fall as elections approach where state capacity is low and races are uncertain (Fails 2025). One case study dissents, arguing that where prices are deregulated, governments have little practical ability to deliver on promises of cheaper fuel (Yeboah-Assiamah, Kundi, and Asamoah 2024). This paper defines election timing as a national election held in a given year, which separates the timing of shielding from its tools.

    Net importer status is the first economic explanation. Where a country produces most of its own gasoline, domestic refiners rather than imports set the price, and governments of producing countries often share resource wealth with citizens through cheap fuel (Cheon, Urpelainen, and Lackner 2013; Fattouh and El-Katiri 2013); a shift from exporter to importer can also change how a government prices fuel (Ichsan, Lockwood, and Ramadhani 2022). Where imports are the marginal supply, the full world cost of the fuel should reach the pump. Research diverges on whether supply conditions matter for gasoline specifically, since some evidence finds that they shape the incidence of diesel taxes but not of gasoline taxes (Marion and Muehlegger 2011). This paper defines net importer status by whether a country's gasoline imports exceed its exports by value.

    Currency regime is the second economic explanation. Because oil and most traded goods are priced in US dollars, a currency that loses value raises the local cost of imported fuel, but retail prices set in local currency adjust only partly and with a delay, so the price consumers pay falls when measured in dollars (Goldberg and Knetter 1997; Campa and Goldberg 2005; Gopinath et al. 2020), and depreciation reduces how fully world fuel prices reach the pump (Kpodar and Imam 2021). A pegged currency, one held fixed to another currency such as the US dollar or the euro, removes much of this movement, keeping the dollar price of fuel steadier and higher. The literature disagrees on how to classify regimes, since many countries that claim to float in fact manage their exchange rates, and many pegs eventually break (Calvo and Reinhart 2002; Ilzetzki, Reinhart, and Rogoff 2019). This paper therefore defines currency regime by observed behavior rather than official declarations, coding a currency as pegged when it is fixed to, or tightly managed against, another currency; pegs to the US dollar specifically serve as an alternative.

    Hypotheses

    Hypothesis 5: Given a national election, we expect a decrease in the price of gasoline.

    The fifth hypothesis concerns the timing of shielding. Fuel prices are visible to nearly every voter, and governments facing an election have strong incentives to prevent them from rising. In an election year, governments should be more willing to hold prices down, delay increases, or cut fuel taxes. The price of gasoline should therefore be lower in years when a national election is held.

    Hypothesis 6: Given net importer status, we expect an increase in the price of gasoline.

    The sixth hypothesis is economic and concerns the structure of the domestic fuel market. In countries that import more gasoline than they export, imports are the marginal source of supply, and the full world cost of the fuel should reach the pump. In countries that meet most of their needs through domestic refining, governments are more likely to share resource wealth through cheap fuel. The price of gasoline should therefore be higher in net importing countries.

    Hypothesis 7: Given a pegged currency, we expect an increase in the price of gasoline.

    The final hypothesis is also economic and concerns the structure of the currency. Where a currency floats and loses value, the dollar price of fuel sold in local currency falls, because retail prices adjust only partly and slowly. A pegged currency removes much of this movement. The price of gasoline, measured in dollars, should therefore be higher where the currency is pegged.

    Data and Measures

    Election timing comes from the Varieties of Democracy project (Coppedge et al. 2026) and is coded 1 if a national election was held that year. Net importer status comes from the UN Comtrade Database (United Nations 2026), which reports the value of each country's gasoline imports and exports each year, and a country is coded 1 if its imports exceed its exports by value. Finally, currency regime is measured with the exchange rate classification of Ilzetzki, Reinhart, and Rogoff (2019), which places each currency in a regime each month according to how it actually behaves rather than how its government describes it. A currency is coded 1 if it was held under a hard peg, a narrow band, or a crawling peg in any month that year, and pegs with the US dollar as the anchor serve as an alternative.

    Findings

    A national election does not lower the price of gasoline, so Hypothesis 5 is not supported. The coefficient is negative in every model but never significant. This is consistent with work finding that election effects on fuel prices are conditional, appearing only where state capacity is low and races are uncertain (Fails 2025), only at very high or very low levels of fuel demand (Klomp 2020), or only where governments control prices (Yeboah-Assiamah, Kundi, and Asamoah 2024). A yearly measure may also be too blunt, since price relief timed in the months before an election would be averaged across the whole year.

    Net importer status has no effect on the price of gasoline, so Hypothesis 6 is not supported. Net importers have lower prices before any controls, but the difference disappears once national income is taken into account. This is consistent with evidence that supply conditions shape the incidence of fuel taxes for diesel but not for gasoline (Marion and Muehlegger 2011). It also suggests that the producer effect found in the literature (Cheon, Urpelainen, and Lackner 2013; Fattouh and El-Katiri 2013) operates through state ownership of the oil sector and regional patterns of subsidy rather than through a country's trade balance in gasoline.

    A pegged currency does not raise the price of gasoline, so Hypothesis 7 is not supported. Currency depreciation, by contrast, lowers the dollar price of gasoline in the full model (Table 3). It also fits the argument that official exchange rate labels can misstate how currencies behave (Calvo and Reinhart 2002). For the price of gasoline, what a currency does appears to matter more than the regime it is placed in.
